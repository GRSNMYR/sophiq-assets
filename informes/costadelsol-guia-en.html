Buyer’s guide

Buying on the Costa del Sol: the questions we asked ourselves first

Before buying here ourselves, we asked the questions you are probably asking now: what it really costs, what to check before signing, and whether this market still has room. We answered them by cross-checking five public sources against our own database of listings. This is what we found.

First things first: what buying really costs

It is the first question almost every foreign buyer asks, and the one that tends to be worst explained. On top of the price of the house, expect roughly:

In practice, budget between 10% and 13% on top of the price for a standard purchase. With a mortgage, a little more.

These are indicative figures for Andalusia and they vary case by case, so confirm them with your lawyer. What matters is having them in mind from the start, because they decide how much you can really pay for the house.

What to check before you sign

This is what we check ourselves. If you are buying on your own, make sure your lawyer checks it.

That what is built matches what is registered

This is the classic problem in Spain. An enclosed terrace, an extra bedroom, a basement or a pool that exist physically but do not appear on the deed or the cadastre. It creates issues with title, valuation, insurance and above all resale, and it can complicate financing.

The community rules

In gated developments and apartment buildings, the community can restrict renovations and, in particular, short term letting. Many buyers look at the house and never look at the rules of the building. That is where the surprise comes from.

The tourist licence, if you plan to let

Never assume it. A house being near the beach and attractive to holidaymakers does not mean it can legally be let by the night. It depends on Andalusian regulations, on the area, and on what the community allows. Confirm it before buying, not after.

The annual cost of owning it

Community fees, council tax (IBI), insurance, utilities, maintenance and, if you will not be there, management. On a second home this weighs on day to day life far more than people expect at the start.

What you get here that you do not get in Madrid

At the prime end, the square metre costs almost the same in both places: around €9,640 on the coast against €10,556 in Madrid. At the very top of the market, Marbella already overtakes Madrid.

The difference is what that square metre buys you. The median prime property on the coast is 695 m² against 232 m² in Madrid. In Madrid, prime means a large apartment or a penthouse. Here it means a villa with land. At a similar price per metre, what multiplies is the ticket: the median prime property is around €3.75M here against €1.56M in Madrid.

There are big differences within the area. Benahavís is the villa municipality, with plots of over 1,000 m² at its top end. Estepona is the most apartment led of the three, and even so it sells homes considerably larger than Madrid’s.

Who buys here, and why it matters to you

Two in every three purchases are made by a foreign buyer, against 14% in Madrid. And no single nationality dominates: the United Kingdom leads, followed by the Netherlands, Poland, Sweden and Germany, and none reaches 15%.

On top of that, one in three registered residents is already foreign. If you are coming from abroad, you will be neither an oddity nor dependent on someone translating daily life for you.

One figure usually goes unnoticed and works in a private buyer’s favour: here only 22% of prime purchases are made through a company, against 48% in prime Madrid. This is a market of people buying homes, not companies buying assets. You are competing with other families, not with funds.

The practical takeaway: prices are set by international demand rather than the local economy, which gives stability against Spanish cycles. And the house that resells well is the one that appeals to that buyer, not the one a local would look for.

What makes a house worth more here

The criteria are not those of a city. In Madrid the neighbourhood and the building decide; here other things do, and they are worth knowing because they are what holds the price when it is your turn to sell.

  • Distance to the sea. The single biggest factor. Between front line and inland there can be up to a 39% difference in value.
  • The plot, the light and a south facing aspect. A villa with land and good orientation does not compete with the same villa facing the wrong way.
  • The setting: golf, marinas, beach clubs. Not just lifestyle. It is what keeps demand alive all year in that particular pocket.
  • Privacy. A gated development and a well run community. People pay for it here.

Two houses ten minutes apart can behave very differently on resale for any one of these four reasons.

The market has risen, but not evenly

Asking prices went from €2,080 per square metre in 2015 to €4,917 in 2025. That is +136%, ahead of Madrid’s +108%.

Read that carefully, because a rise like this can be taken to mean you are already too late. Not quite. The jump is concentrated from 2020 onwards, and until 2019 Madrid was growing faster. Nor has everything risen equally: there are more than forty points between the municipality that rose most and the one that rose least. Getting it right has more to do with what you buy and where than with when you come in.

There is a lot on the market, and it sells slowly

Around 10% of the housing stock is on the market at any one time, against a national average of 2.6%. The average time to sell is 115 days, almost twice Madrid’s.

Selling slowly does not mean selling little: the area is in the national top 4% for transactions per inhabitant, with 35 per thousand against a Spanish average of 13. There is activity, which is what matters the day you want to sell.

With one caveat worth knowing: stock is falling. Active listings have gone from around 50,000 in 2020 to just under 30,000 in 2025. There is still plenty to choose from, but less than five years ago.

And something worth knowing before you start looking: the same house often appears in several places at once, sometimes at different prices. What you see on the market is considerably more than what actually exists, and it is not always easy to tell which listing reflects reality.

What this means for you. A market with plenty of supply and slow absorption works in your favour: there is room to negotiate, and any pressure to hurry is usually manufactured.

The flip side is that searching on your own is exhausting, and comparing prices is harder than it looks.
